Every few years someone announces that business analysts are finished. Low-code was going to do it. Then self-service BI. Now it's AI. I've heard enough of these to be suspicious, but this time part of the claim holds up, so it's worth being specific about which part.

## What's about to get cheap

A lot of BA work is producing documents: meeting notes, user stories, acceptance criteria, process maps, requirements summaries. A language model will write a decent first draft of any of them in seconds. It won't be perfect, but a first draft was never the hard part.

Within three to five years, I expect drafting to be close to free. I also expect stakeholders to notice.

## What won't

The hard part of the job is the bit that never makes it into the documents. It's working out what people actually need, which is rarely what they ask for. They ask for a solution, or a report, or a button. Sometimes they change their mind and tell me later.

A model can summarise the meeting. It can't tell that the person who agreed the loudest has no authority, or that the real requirement was mentioned in the corridor afterwards. That takes context, judgement and a bit of trust, and those are still slow to build.

## What the job becomes

A few shifts I'd bet on:

- **Less writing, more deciding.** The BA becomes the editor of AI-written drafts, and the person accountable for whether they're right.
- **More technical fluency.** Writing acceptance criteria for a system that gives a different answer every time is a genuinely new problem. Someone has to define what "good enough" means, and test for it.
- **Closer to product.** I've worked on both sides of that line, and it's already blurry. With drafting automated, the remaining work is mostly about problems, priorities and trade-offs, which is product work by another name.
- **Shorter loops.** If a working prototype takes an afternoon, requirements get tested by showing people something instead of describing it to them.

## What I'd do now

- Use the tools on your own drafting, and learn where they quietly make things up.
- Get better at the questions, not the templates.
- Keep a record of decisions and the reasons behind them. It's the thing these tools are worst at knowing.

I could be wrong. I've been wrong about timelines before. Check back in 2030 and see how I went.
